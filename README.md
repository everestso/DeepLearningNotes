## Witches of Butler Park

Reading about Sweden’s great witch trials of 1668–1676, known as *“The Great Noise,”* brings back an unlikely memory: my own introduction to witchcraft in fourth grade, at the age of nine ('71). Children’s testimony played a central role in those trials, with adults giving authority to stories that helped send accused people to their deaths. The consequences were far removed from anything in my childhood, but the accounts make me think about how frightening stories circulate among children—and how compelling they can become.

My introduction began with Bloody Mary. Someone explained that you could make her appear by standing before a mirror and repeating an incantation three times. I remember the beginning as “Bloody Mary in the mirror,” but the rest has slipped away. What remains is the premise: say the words, follow the instructions, and something might appear.

From there, the games grew more elaborate. There was talk of a levitation spell, and several children attempted to levitate a fellow student inside a closet. I did not witness the attempt, so I cannot say exactly what happened in that closet. What I can say, is that I have never forgotten being in the house when this spell was attempted in the closet, and how the story of it became part of the community experience—the claim that children had tried something extraordinary, somewhere out of sight.

The games culminated in an incident involving a student who was persuaded to enter a closet and recite the incantation. The student emerged crying, bringing the fear into the classroom. Whatever had been intended as a game, the distress was real.

Looking back, I cannot know what each child believed, or how much was curiosity, performance, or pressure to go along. I remember fragments of words, an unwitnessed levitation attempt, and a frightened classmate. Those memories give me a small point of connection to the Swedish history: children can frighten one another through stories and shared expectations. In Sweden, adults and institutions gave such stories a terrible power. In our fourth-grade classroom, the consequences were smaller, but a child still came out of a closet in tears.

An unsettling connection emerged as I considered the Swedish witch trials, in which children claimed they could identify witches and accused people in their own communities. The thought that games of witchcraft spreading from child to child could throw entire families into turmoil from their implications of how the knowledge of such things were learned, was ominous. 

Such memories are as vivid as the carefree introduction of summer neighborhood BookMobile By classmates.

The Sixth International Workshop on Machine Learning will be held at Cornell University from June 29 through July 1, 1989. The workshop will be divided into six parallel sessions, each focusing on a different theme:

### Combining Empirical and Explanation-Based Learning
**M. Pazzani, chair**

Both empirical evaluation and theoretical analysis have been used to identify the strengths and weaknesses of individual learning methods. Integrated approaches to learning have the potential of overcoming the limitations of individual methods. Papers are solicited exploring hybrid techniques involving, for example, explanation-based learning, case-based reasoning, constructive induction, or neural networks.

### Empirical Learning: Theory and Application
**C. Sammut, chair**

This session will be devoted to discussions on inductive (also called empirical) learning with particular emphasis on results that can be justified by theory or experimental evaluation. Papers should characterize methodologies (either formally or experimentally), their performance and/or problems for which they are well/ill suited. Comparative studies applying different methodologies to the same problem are also invited.

### Learning Plan Knowledge
**S. Chien and G. DeJong, co-chairs**

This session will explore machine learning of plan-related knowledge; specifically, learning to construct, index, and recognize plans by using explanation-based, empirical, case-based, analogical, and connectionist approaches.

### Knowledge-Base Refinement and Theory Revision
**A. Ginsberg, chair**

Knowledge-base refinement involves the discovery of plausible refinements to a knowledge base in order to improve the breadth and accuracy of the associated expert system. More generally, theory revision is concerned with systems that start out having some domain theory, but one that is incomplete and fallible. Two basic problems are how to use an imperfect theory to guide one in learning more about the domain as more experience accumulates, and how to use the knowledge so gained to revise the theory in appropriate ways.

### Incremental Learning
**D. Fisher, chair, with J. Grefenstette, J. Schlimmer, R. Sutton, and P. Utgoff**

Incremental learning requires continuous adaptation to the environment subject to performance constraints of timely response, environmental assumptions such as noise or concept drift, and knowledge base limitations. Papers that cross traditionally disparate paradigms are highly encouraged, notably rule-based, connectionist, and genetic learning; explanation-based and inductive learning; procedure and concept learning; psychological and computational theories of learning; and belief revision, bounded rationality, and learning.

### Representational Issues in Machine Learning
**D. Subramanian, chair**

This session will study representational practice in machine learning in order to understand the relationship between inference (inductive and deductive) and choice of representation. Present-day learners depend on careful vocabulary engineering for their success. What is the nature of the contribution representation makes to learning, and how can we make learners design/redesign hypotheses languages automatically? Papers are solicited in areas including, but not limited to, bias, representation change and reformulation, and knowledge-level analysis of learning algorithms.

## Early Narratives: What is in a name, “Fresno”

**“Fresno” is Spanish for “ash tree.”** The name **Fresno River** was in use by the early 1850s, although its exact naming date is uncertain.

- **1806:** Gabriel Moraga’s expedition called the stream **Santa Ana**, an earlier Spanish name identified by historian Sherburne F. Cook with today’s Fresno River.
- **Around 1851:** According to KVPR’s historical account, **Fresno River** began appearing on maps of the region.
- **1852:** A surviving Gold Rush letter is headed **“Frezno river California,”** providing contemporary evidence of the name.
- **1856:** **Fresno County** took its name from the river and the ash trees along its banks.
- **1893:** The creation of **Madera County** placed the Fresno River outside the remaining Fresno County.

“Fresno Creek” has not been established here as an earlier official name.

# Spirit of DownTown Fresno

**Downtown Fresno began as a railroad settlement in 1872 and grew into the San Joaquin Valley’s commercial and civic center.** Its history reflects agricultural prosperity, immigrant communities, suburban expansion, and repeated efforts at renewal.

- **1872–1885: Railroad beginnings.** The Central Pacific Railroad established Fresno Station in **1872**, drawing businesses and residents around the depot. Fresno replaced Millerton as the **county seat in 1874** and incorporated in **1885**.

- **Late 1800s–early 1900s: A growing, diverse center.** Agricultural development supported downtown stores, banks, hotels, and warehouses. **Chinatown developed west of the railroad tracks**, while Armenian residents established businesses and neighborhoods southeast of the commercial core. Discrimination and residential restrictions shaped these communities’ geography.

- **1890s–1930s: Downtown’s heyday.** Streetcars, introduced in **1892**, connected neighborhoods to bustling shopping streets. Landmark buildings included the **Pacific Southwest Building (1925)** and **Pantages Theatre, now Warnors (1928)**. Fulton Street became the region’s principal shopping corridor.

- **1950s–1970s: Suburban competition and urban renewal.** Housing and shopping expanded northward, weakening downtown’s retail dominance. To attract shoppers, Fresno opened the **Fulton Mall on September 1, 1964**—six blocks converted into a pedestrian landscape designed by **Garrett Eckbo**, within a broader plan by **Victor Gruen’s firm**. Its fountains, sculptures, and gardens earned recognition, but suburban competition continued.

- **Late 1900s onward: Decline and reinvestment.** Downtown experienced retail departures and vacancies. More recent revitalization efforts emphasize historic preservation, housing, arts, restaurants, and entertainment. **Fulton Street reopened to vehicles on October 21, 2017**, marking another major change in the effort to revive Fresno’s historic center.

## Tramp Nightclub: History, Founders, and Connection to Ghislaine Maxwell

**Tramp** opened on **18 December 1969** at **40 Jermyn Street, St James’s, London**. Named after Charlie Chaplin’s “Tramp” character, it became a celebrated private nightclub where royalty, film stars, and musicians could socialize with considerable privacy. Its founders were **Johnny Gold, Oscar Lerman, and Bill Ofner**.

### The Founders

- **Johnny Gold (1932–2021)** became the club’s public face and principal host. A former bookmaker, he had previously managed **Dolly’s**, a fashionable London nightclub owned by Lerman. At Tramp, Gold cultivated personal relationships with celebrities and enforced rules against photography and autograph hunting. He sold his interest in **1998**, but continued welcoming guests until **2003**, before retiring to the Bahamas.

- **Oscar Lerman (1919–1992)** was an American nightclub entrepreneur and film producer, and the husband of novelist **Jackie Collins**, whom he married in **1965**. He encouraged her writing and later produced adaptations including *The Stud* (1978) and *The Bitch* (1979). His entertainment connections helped link Tramp to the Collins sisters and their celebrity circle; nightclub scenes in *The Stud*, starring Joan Collins, were filmed there.

- **Bill Ofner** was the third founding business partner and is less extensively documented in published accounts. Jackie Collins credited him with finding the premises, describing their division of responsibilities: **“Bill Ofner found it, Oscar Lerman designed it and Johnny Gold ran it.”**

Their combination of hospitality, entertainment connections, and discretion helped make Tramp a fixture of London nightlife, attracting figures such as **Frank Sinatra, Mick Jagger, Michael Caine, and Joan Collins**.

### Connection to Ghislaine Maxwell

The club’s connection to **Ghislaine Maxwell** became widely publicized through **Virginia Giuffre’s allegations against Prince Andrew**. Giuffre said that, in **March 2001**, aged **17**, she visited Tramp with Andrew, Jeffrey Epstein, and Maxwell. She alleged that she danced with Andrew and that Maxwell subsequently directed her to have sex with him at Maxwell’s London townhouse.

Andrew denied her account and settled her civil lawsuit in **2022 without admitting liability**. These allegations did not establish wrongdoing by Tramp or its founders.

Maxwell was separately convicted in **2021** of crimes connected to Epstein’s sexual abuse of minors and sentenced to **20 years in prison in 2022**. That conviction was distinct from Giuffre’s specific allegations about the evening at Tramp.

# WITCH

One of the most revealing aspects of the European witch trials was the close association between **female sexuality, moral transgression, and supernatural evil**. From the late Middle Ages through the seventeenth century, theologians, religious authorities, and witch hunters increasingly characterized women as particularly vulnerable to the Devil because of their supposedly uncontrolled sexual desires.

This association was not incidental. In some influential accounts of witchcraft, **female sexual immorality became both a sign of demonic influence and a supposed pathway to becoming a witch.**

## 1. Medieval Christianity: Women, Eve, and Sexual Temptation

The association had roots in longstanding Christian interpretations of the biblical story of Adam and Eve.

In certain theological traditions, Eve's temptation by the serpent became evidence of women's supposed greater vulnerability to deception, weakness, and moral corruption.

These ideas contributed to a view of women as:

- More vulnerable than men to temptation and manipulation.
- Particularly susceptible to sexual desire.
- Capable of leading men into sin through seduction.
- In need of supervision by husbands, fathers, and religious authorities.

These beliefs were not universal within Christianity, but they became influential in the intellectual tradition that shaped European witchcraft prosecutions.

## 2. The *Malleus Maleficarum* (1486–1487): Sexual Desire as a Pathway to Witchcraft

<img src="Malleus_Maleficarum.jpg" width="220" alt="Historical title page of the Malleus Maleficarum">

The *Malleus Maleficarum* (*Hammer of Witches*), principally written by the Dominican inquisitor Heinrich Kramer, became one of the most notorious texts associated with the European witch persecutions.

Kramer argued that women were especially susceptible to witchcraft because of their supposedly greater lust, weakness, and inclination toward deception.

Historian Hans Peter Broedel identifies fear of uncontrolled female sexuality as central to Kramer's construction of the witch.

In this framework, women's sexual desire was not simply considered a personal moral weakness. It was portrayed as a potential opening through which Satan could exert influence.

Kramer also alleged that witches could cause impotence, interfere with reproduction, and manipulate relationships through supernatural means.

The consequence was a particularly dangerous circular logic: a woman suspected of excessive sexual desire could be considered vulnerable to witchcraft, while an accusation of witchcraft could itself become evidence of sexual depravity.

## 3. Sexual Intercourse with the Devil

During the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, European demonologists elaborated on the belief that witches entered into sexual relationships with Satan.

The alleged relationship typically involved three elements:

1. **A pact with the Devil:** The accused supposedly renounced Christianity in exchange for supernatural powers.
2. **Sexual submission:** The witch was alleged to have intercourse with Satan or a male demon known as an *incubus*.
3. **Membership in a witches' sabbath:** Witches were supposedly participants in secret nighttime gatherings involving devil worship, feasting, dancing, and sexual transgression.

These stories developed into an elaborate mythology in which sexual activity represented a violation of both Christian morality and the established social order.

Importantly, historians distinguish these allegations from actual behavior. Many surviving confessions were obtained through torture, coercion, and leading questions. They do not establish that the alleged events occurred.

## 4. Sexual Immorality and the Social Position of Accused Women

Accusations were sometimes linked to women's real or perceived departures from accepted sexual and domestic roles.

| Perceived behavior | Possible interpretation by contemporaries |
|---|---|
| Adultery or extramarital relationships | Evidence of uncontrolled lust or moral corruption |
| Prostitution | Association with sexual sin and potentially illicit magic |
| Sexual independence | Challenge to patriarchal expectations |
| Love spells or sexual magic | Attempt to manipulate another person's desire or fidelity |
| Knowledge of contraception or reproductive practices | Potential suspicion of interference with God's intended reproductive order |
| Refusal to conform to expected domestic roles | Evidence of disorderly or rebellious character |

Historical research on medieval love magic documents the particular association between women, illicit relationships, and magical practices intended to influence sexual attraction.

However, **sexual misconduct was not a necessary condition for a witchcraft accusation**. Many accused women were married, elderly, socially respectable, or regarded as devout Christians. Neighborhood conflicts, unexplained deaths, illness, economic disputes, and longstanding reputations could be equally or more important.

## 5. The Sexualized Image of the Witch

<img src="Hans_Baldung_Grien_Witches.jpg" width="400" alt="Hans Baldung Grien's early-sixteenth-century depiction of witches">

*Early-sixteenth-century depictions associated with Hans Baldung Grien illustrate how the European witch became a sexualized figure in art and popular imagination.*

The visual representation of witches often incorporated nudity, sexual disorder, and the inversion of conventional femininity.

This imagery reinforced the notion that witches occupied a world in which ordinary Christian morality no longer applied. Sexual freedom, religious rebellion, and supernatural power became intertwined.

The witch could therefore represent several perceived threats simultaneously: a woman who rejected religious authority, transgressed sexual restrictions, and possessed power outside accepted male-controlled institutions.

## 6. The American Witch Trials: Salem and Puritan Sexual Morality

<img src="Examination_of_a_Witch_Matteson.jpg" width="400" alt="Examination of a Witch by Tompkins H. Matteson, 1853">

The **Salem witch trials of 1692** inherited many European beliefs about women's susceptibility to Satan.

However, the American accusations generally emphasized covenants with the Devil, spiritual corruption, and supernatural attacks more than the elaborate sexual orgies described in some continental European witch trials.

Approximately 78–80% of those accused at Salem were women.

Historian Elizabeth Reis explores this relationship in her book *Damned Women: Sinners and Witches in Puritan New England* (Cornell University Press, 1997).

Reis argues that Puritan beliefs about sin, the Devil, and female moral vulnerability help explain why women were disproportionately accused and why some women confessed.

In Puritan New England, the association was often less explicitly sexual than in continental European demonology. Nevertheless, beliefs about female weakness, disobedience, and vulnerability to spiritual corruption remained important.

## 7. Witchcraft as a Means of Regulating Female Behavior

One influential historical interpretation is that witchcraft accusations could function as a mechanism of social control.

A woman who challenged the expectations of her community—whether through sexual conduct, economic independence, interpersonal conflict, or unconventional behavior—might become especially vulnerable to suspicion.

The following pattern illustrates the relationship:

**Social expectations of women**

*Chastity, obedience, marriage, domestic responsibility*

↓

**Perceived violation of expectations**

*Sexual misconduct, independence, interpersonal disputes*

↓

**Suspicion of moral or spiritual corruption**

↓

**Potential witchcraft accusation**

*This is an interpretive model of one possible pathway to accusation, not a universal sequence followed in historical trials.*

Modern historians caution against interpreting the witch trials exclusively as a campaign against sexually independent women. Men were also prosecuted, and in some regions they formed a substantial proportion of the accused.

The evidence points to complex interactions among gender, theology, community conflict, legal institutions, and political instability.

## Historical Conclusion

The connection between female sexual immorality and witchcraft reflects a larger historical struggle over religious authority, sexuality, and women's position in society.

The witch was not merely imagined as someone who practiced forbidden magic. In important strands of European demonology, she was portrayed as a woman whose uncontrolled desires made her susceptible to Satan and whose resulting supernatural powers threatened the established social order.

**The central historical irony is that sexual immorality could be treated simultaneously as a cause of witchcraft and as evidence that witchcraft had occurred.** This reasoning made accusations extraordinarily difficult to disprove.

The European trials thus reveal not only the fear of supernatural forces, but also deep anxieties concerning female sexuality, bodily autonomy, and women's capacity to exercise power outside traditional institutions.

## Alberto Maria Segre

**Alberto Maria Segre** is a computer scientist whose career connects early artificial intelligence and machine learning with computational biology and public health. He is a professor and chair of Computer Science at the **University of Iowa**, where he holds the **Gerard P. Weeg Faculty Scholar in Informatics** appointment.

### Background and Career

- **Music and engineering:** He earned a BA in Music Theory and BS, MS, and PhD degrees in Electrical Engineering at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, completing his doctorate in **1987**.
- **Cornell and early AI:** Before joining Iowa in **1994**, he was an assistant professor at Cornell University. His early research included machine learning, automated planning, and robotic assembly.
- **Distributed computing and biology:** He developed distributed search and optimization methods, with applications including protein structure prediction and locating disease-associated genetic mutations.

### Computational Epidemiology

Segre co-founded Iowa’s **Computational Epidemiology Research Group**, alongside physician **Philip Polgreen**. Their work uses computational models and simulations to study disease transmission, particularly in hospitals, and inform disease surveillance and prevention.

In **2026**, he received Iowa’s **Regents Award for Faculty Excellence**, recognizing his research, teaching, and university service, including 16 years as department chair.

### Connection to ML ’92 in Aberdeen

Segre was at Cornell when he attended the **Ninth International Conference on Machine Learning**, held in **Aberdeen, Scotland, July 1–3, 1992**.

He wrote **“The Ninth International Conference on Machine Learning,”** published in *AI Magazine*, **14(2), pages 16–17, Summer 1993**. His report confirms that the conference included an **evening poster session**, although it does not specify the day.

## Origins of European Witch Hunts

European witch hunts developed gradually: **belief in magic was ancient, but large-scale trials for a supposed conspiracy of devil-worshipping witches emerged much later.**

- **Ancient Europe:** Greek, Roman, Celtic, and Germanic societies practiced divination, healing rituals, protective charms, and curses. Authorities sometimes punished harmful magic, while other supernatural practices were accepted parts of religion and everyday life.

- **Early Christian Europe, roughly 400–1000:** Church authorities condemned many magical practices as pagan or superstitious. Some influential church teaching treated supposed nighttime flights with supernatural beings as delusions rather than actual events.

- **Medieval Europe, roughly 1000–1300:** Folk healing, charms, astrology, and learned ritual magic continued. Campaigns against heresy helped develop investigative institutions and ideas about organized enemies of Christianity that later influenced witch prosecutions.

- **The turning point, roughly 1300–1450:** Some theologians and legal authorities increasingly connected harmful magic with **deliberate pacts with the Devil**. Separate beliefs about curses, demonic assistance, nighttime gatherings, and flight began merging into a broader concept of witchcraft.

- **Early organized hunts, 1420s–1430s:** Major clusters of prosecutions appeared in the **western Alpine region**, including parts of present-day Switzerland and France. The **Valais trials beginning in 1428** are an important early example.

- **Women and the “weaker vessel”:** Some witchcraft writers drew on misogynistic traditions and interpretations of Scripture to argue that women were weaker in faith and reason, and therefore more susceptible to temptation and witchcraft. They invoked Eve’s temptation and biblical language describing the wife as the **“weaker vessel” (1 Peter 3:7)**. **The verse itself does not associate women with witchcraft**; that connection was an interpretation used to justify prejudice. The passage also instructs husbands to honor their wives as fellow heirs of divine grace.

- **The *Malleus Maleficarum*, 1486–1487:** Written by **Heinrich Kramer**, a German Dominican inquisitor also known as **Henricus Institoris**, this treatise reinforced claims that women were especially susceptible to witchcraft. **Jacob Sprenger** is traditionally credited as co-author, but his involvement is disputed. The title means **“Hammer of Witches”**: *malleus* means “hammer,” and *maleficarum* means “of female evildoers,” conventionally translated as “of witches.” The feminine plural explicitly identifies women.

- **The largest hunts, roughly 1560–1650:** Witch prosecutions intensified amid religious conflict, political tensions, and local crises. Women constituted the majority of those prosecuted across Europe, although the proportion varied considerably and men predominated in some regions.

The crucial change was the growing claim that an accused person belonged to a **collective, satanic threat**. This encouraged investigators to seek accomplices and allowed individual accusations to become mass prosecutions. **Gender prejudice helped determine who was most likely to be suspected.**

## Brief History of Germany and the Holy Roman Empire

**Germany became a unified nation-state in 1871, but its historical roots lie in the German-speaking territories of the Holy Roman Empire.** The empire’s territory differed considerably from modern Germany.

| Period | Brief History and Territory |
|---|---|
| **Ancient era–800** | Various Germanic peoples inhabited central Europe. Rome controlled areas west of the Rhine and south of the Danube. Later, the **Frankish kingdom** united much of western and central Europe; Charlemagne became emperor in **800**. |
| **843–962: East Francia** | The division of Charlemagne’s empire in **843** created **East Francia**, the principal political predecessor of medieval Germany. Its territories included much of present-day Germany and neighboring regions. |
| **962–1806: Holy Roman Empire** | Conventionally dated from **Otto I’s imperial coronation in 962**, the empire centered on German lands. At different times it also included **Austria, Bohemia (much of today’s Czech Republic), the Low Countries, Switzerland, parts of eastern France, and northern and central Italy**. Its borders changed considerably. |
| **Medieval and early modern empire** | The empire became a patchwork of duchies, principalities, bishoprics, and free cities under an elected emperor. **It was never a unified German nation-state.** The Reformation beginning in **1517** deepened religious divisions; the Thirty Years’ War (**1618–1648**) devastated many German territories. |
| **1806–1871: Toward unification** | Napoleon’s victories brought the empire’s dissolution in **1806**. The **German Confederation**, established in **1815**, loosely associated 39 states, including Austria and Prussia through their participating territories. Their rivalry ended with Prussia leading unification and excluding Austria. |
| **1871–1918: German Empire** | The German Empire was proclaimed in **1871** under the Prussian king. It included most of modern Germany, **Alsace-Lorraine**, and extensive eastern territories now in Poland and Russia. Austria remained separate. |
| **1918–1945: Republic and dictatorship** | Defeat in World War I brought territorial losses and the **Weimar Republic**. Hitler took power in **1933**. Nazi Germany perpetrated the Holocaust and pursued territorial expansion, invading Poland in **1939**, beginning World War II in Europe. Germany was defeated in **1945**. |
| **1945–1990: Division** | Germany’s former territories east of the **Oder–Neisse line** passed predominantly to Poland, while northern East Prussia came under Soviet control. Allied occupation led to the establishment of **West Germany and East Germany in 1949**. |
| **1990–present: Reunified Germany** | East and West Germany reunited on **October 3, 1990**. Germany confirmed its existing borders, including its border with Poland, and continued as a federal parliamentary democracy within the European Union. |

**Connection to the witch trials:** During the main European witch-trial period, “Germany” generally refers to the numerous German territories within the **Holy Roman Empire**. Their fragmented courts and governments help explain why prosecutions varied so sharply from one locality to another.
